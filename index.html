The curricula we teach from

Tap any program to read what it is and why we chose it.

Amplify Core Knowledge Language Arts, 3rd Edition

English · Kindergarten to Grade 4

CKLA teaches English in two halves that run side by side. The Skills strand builds reading from the sound up: letter sounds first, then blending, then spelling patterns, so that a child decodes words rather than guessing at them. The Knowledge strand is read aloud by the teacher and is deliberately harder than what a child can yet read alone, covering fairy tales, the human body, ancient civilizations, astronomy and more.

We chose it because the research is unambiguous on two points. Reading is taught most reliably through systematic phonics, and comprehension depends less on strategy lessons than on how much a child already knows about the world. CKLA takes both seriously at once. It is demanding, and it asks a great deal of the teacher, which is why our staff are given full lesson materials for it.

Savvas myView Literacy and myPerspectives

English · Grade 5 uses myView, Grades 6 to 8 use myPerspectives Virginia 2026

From Grade 5 the work shifts from learning to read toward reading to learn. myView keeps a structured reading and writing block while widening the range of texts. In the middle grades, myPerspectives is organized around essential questions, with each unit pairing literature and informational texts so that students argue from evidence rather than from opinion.

We chose the Virginia 2026 edition of myPerspectives specifically because it is written to the Virginia English Standards of Learning rather than adapted to them afterwards. Novels are added to each year at the school’s choosing, so that alongside the program our students read whole books.

Savvas enVision Mathematics

Mathematics · Kindergarten to Grade 8

enVision teaches each concept three times over: first with concrete objects, then with a picture or model, and only then with the written method. Every lesson opens with a problem the class attempts before being shown a method, which is what makes the later explanation land. Mathematical vocabulary is taught explicitly rather than assumed.

We use the national edition of enVision, and we are candid about what that means. It is written to the national standards, not to Virginia’s, and the two do not match exactly. Where Virginia asks for something the program does not teach, our teachers work from a school-written curriculum guide that names the gap and supplies the lesson. Where the program teaches beyond the Virginia grade level, we mark it as enrichment rather than spend weeks on it. This is the reason our curriculum maps exist.

Five Ponds Press

Science and Social Studies · Kindergarten to Grade 4

Five Ponds Press writes specifically for Virginia. Their Social Studies titles move outward year by year, from the child’s own community to the Commonwealth and then the nation, and the Grade 4 book is Virginia Studies in full. The Science titles, Exploring Science All Around Us, follow the Virginia Science Standards of Learning strand by strand.

We chose them for the youngest grades because the books are short, plainly written and genuinely readable by the children themselves, rather than being teacher texts with pictures. That matters when a seven year old is being asked to read for information for the first time.

Savvas Experience Science

Science · Grade 3, Grade 5, and Earth and Physical Science in the middle grades

Experience Science is built around investigation. Each unit opens with a phenomenon students have to explain, and the content arrives as they need it to build that explanation. Students are asked to make a claim, support it with evidence and reason from that evidence, which is the habit the middle school and high school science standards are built on.

We supplement it with school-written material where the Virginia standards ask for something the program treats lightly, and our teachers are given science background written from the ground up so that the subject can be taught confidently by a non-specialist.

Savvas myWorld Interactive Social Studies

Social Studies · Grades 5 to 8

myWorld carries United States history across Grades 5 and 6, from the earliest peoples through to the present day, and world history in Grades 7 and 8. Each topic is built around primary sources, maps and a guiding question, so that students handle evidence rather than only a narrative.

Where the program treats a people or a period too briefly, our teachers extend it deliberately, and our curriculum maps say exactly where.

School-written materials

Across every subject, where a published program falls short

No published program matches the Virginia standards perfectly, and none of them was written for our school. Rather than teach a book cover to cover and hope, we map every program against the standards, find the gaps, and write what is missing ourselves.

In practice that means teacher playbooks, student packets, review sheets and assessments produced in-house and kept in a curriculum folder for each grade. It also means every family guide on this site is written from those maps, so what parents read matches what is taught.

PLEASE READ THIS FIRST

This guide is a plan, not a promise. Pre-K works in ten units of about four weeks each, and the dates are approximate. A unit stretches or shortens as the class needs it to. Where your child is going by June will not change.

The teaching day runs 8:45 to 12:00, with a rest period from 12:00 to 2:00. There are no tests in Pre-K: your child is assessed by watching them work, and you receive a report every quarter.

HOW YOUR CHILD IS ASSESSED

There are no tests in Pre-K. The teacher watches your child work and writes it down on the day. Five windows: a starting point in early September, a check in late October, a full mid-year check in January, a check at the end of March, and a Kindergarten readiness check in June.

A report comes home every quarter, and conferences fall on 2 November and 29 January. In June a portfolio comes home holding three dated samples of your child writing their own name, from September, January and June, so you can see the year in their own hand.

BY JUNE, WITH PRACTICE AT HOME

School alone will not get your child here. Counting, talking and reading together at home matters more now than at any later age.

  • Write their own name, holding the pencil properly
  • Recognize the capital letters and know most of their sounds
  • Count to 20, and count a set of ten objects accurately
  • Write the numbers 1 to 10
  • Listen to a whole story and answer a question about it
  • Say what they think in a sentence somebody else can follow
  • Wait, share, and greet each other by name

WHICH IS WHY THESE SIX THINGS MATTER

  • Read aloud every day. The same book again is fine.
  • Count real things together: stairs, grapes, shoes.
  • Let them hold a crayon every day. Coloring is handwriting practice.
  • Talk in whole sentences, and wait for a whole sentence back.
  • Say the letter and its sound when you see one in the world.
  • If it is getting hard, tell the teacher in October, not April.

Questions are always welcome. Please speak to your child’s teacher or the school office.

Kindergarten English: Reading, Writing and Spelling

A month by month guide for families, 2026 to 2027

Your child learns to read this year in a careful order. First they learn to hear sounds, then which letter makes each sound, then how to put sounds together to read and spell real words. Nothing is taught before your child is ready for it. Use this guide to see what they are working on each month and what helps most at home.

Three things to know before you read on

  • Sounds come before letter names. We say the sound the letter makes, /m/ rather than "em". Saying the sound at home is what helps your child read.
  • Tricky Words are learned by heart. Words like the, said and they cannot be sounded out, so we memorize them. They live on our Tricky Word Wall and are listed for you below.
  • Spelling checks start in October and build to weekly. Two checks in October, three in November and December, then one every Friday from January. Eight words each time, all of them sound-out-able. Please do not drill lists at home. Reading together does more.
AUGUST & SEPT.Units 1 and 2

What your child is learning

Getting ready to read and write. Your child listens closely and tells sounds apart, hears that a sentence is made of separate words, and learns that we read from left to right and top to bottom. They learn to hold a pencil properly and to draw the lines, circles and curves that letters are built from. By late September they are blending sounds out loud: /c/ /a/ /t/ makes cat. Letters come next month.

At home: close your eyes together and name the sounds you hear. Say a short word slowly, sound by sound, and let your child guess it.

Words to know by heart

None yet. Tricky Words begin in October.

Kindergarten: What We Are Learning About

What this half of the day is

Alongside reading and spelling, your child spends about fifty minutes a day listening to the teacher read aloud from books far above what a five year old could read alone. This is deliberate. A child understands what they read only if they already know something about the subject, so we spend the year building that knowledge: science, geography, history, stories and art. Your child will come home using words like pollination, continent and stewardship, and will mean them.

Kindergarten Reading: Every Check, and How to Help

The reading checks from late September onward, and how to help | 2026 to 2027

Before you use this page

  • Ten minutes, a few times a week, is enough. Short and happy beats long and serious at this age. Find the row for the month you are in, read it, and play the ideas together.
  • Sounds, not names. When we say sounds we mean the sound a letter makes, /m/, not its name. The rows tell you which to practice, and in January it switches to names.
  • Tricky Words cannot be sounded out. Words like the, said and was are memorized by sight. Just read them together often.
  • The two diagnostics are not graded. The December and June checks show growth, not marks. There is nothing to cram for, so just keep reading.
  • Praise the effort, not the score. You worked hard sounding that out does more for a five year old than getting every word right.
  • Timings are approximate. Checks move with the class, so this page gives the part of the month rather than a date. Your teacher confirms the exact day on Class Dojo.

Blending, which means pushing sounds together to make a word, and tracing their own first name.

How to help at home

Kindergarten Mathematics: What We Are Learning

How math runs in Kindergarten

Your child does math every day from the enVision program, and alongside it a calendar routine runs every single day of the year. That routine is where the days of the week, the months and telling time are actually learned, so it is not a separate unit you will see on this page. Two short units were added by the school this year to teach things the textbook leaves out: counting and comparing to 30 in February, and the data cycle at the end of May.

Aug 24 to Sep 30Launch, Topics 1 and 2

What your child is learning about

Getting started, and numbers 0 to 5

Launch week sets up the routines, the tools and the daily calendar. Then counting to five: your child learns that the last number you say is the total, and that the total does not change if you move the objects around. They read and write the numerals 0 to 5 and begin comparing two small groups.

New words: count, number, zero, order, more, fewer, same as

Try this at home

Count what is already in front of you. Five fingers, five prayers, five plates.

Oct 1 to Oct 29Topics 3 and 4

What your child is learning about

Numbers 6 to 10, and comparing

Ten becomes the anchor. Your child learns to see 6 to 10 as five and some more using a ten frame, then compares two groups using 5 and 10 as benchmarks instead of counting every single object.

New words: six to ten, greater than, less than, equal to, one more, one fewer

Try this at home

Ask which plate has more without counting. Then count to check.

Oct 30 to Nov 5School Patterns unit

What your child is learning about

Repeating patterns

A short unit added by the school because the textbook does not cover it. Your child learns to spot a repeating pattern, say it out loud, carry it on, and make a new one.

New words: pattern, repeat, continue

Try this at home

Lay out spoons and forks in a pattern and let your child continue it.

Nov 6 to Dec 11Topics 6 and 7

What your child is learning about

Addition and subtraction

Addition joins parts into a whole, and the same whole can be made from different pairs of parts. Subtraction separates a whole or finds a missing part. Your child learns that every subtraction story has an addition story hiding inside it.

New words: join, part, whole, add, plus, equals, sum, subtract, take away, are left,

difference

Try this at home

Use snacks. You have six and give two away. How many are left?

Dec 14 to Dec 22Practice days

What your child is learning about

Going back over it before the break

No new content. These days are deliberately held back so that addition and subtraction are solid before the winter break, rather than half learned.

Try this at home

Keep a counting game going over the break. Little and often beats long sessions.

Jan 5 to Jan 21Topic 8

What your child is learning about

More addition and subtraction

Strategies. Your child learns the pairs that make ten, doubles, counting on and counting back, and that different strategies can reach the same answer. This is the engine of Grade 1 arithmetic.

New words: make 10, missing part, doubles, count on, count back, equation, true,

false

Try this at home

Ask what goes with seven to make ten. Then let your child ask you.

Jan 22 to Feb 11Topic 9 and school unit

What your child is learning about

Counting to 20, then to 30

Teen numbers keep the counting pattern going, and counting on from a number is faster than starting again at one. A short school unit then extends counting and comparing all the way to 30.

New words: eleven to twenty, count on, count back, before, after, between

Try this at home

Count past twenty on the stairs, in the car, anywhere.

What your child is learning about

Teen numbers: one ten and some ones

Every teen number is one ten and some ones. That single idea is what place value is built on, so it is arguably the most important thing your child learns in math this year.

New words: ten and some ones, ones, compose, decompose, teen numbers

Count out fourteen small things, push ten together, and look at the four left over.

Counting to 100

New words: hundred chart, count by tens, skip count, group of ten, row, column,

pattern

Try this at home

Count to a hundred by tens while walking or climbing stairs.

Circles, triangles, squares and rectangles. Your child learns that a shape is named by its attributes, how many sides and corners it has, and not by which way it happens to be turned. They then compare shapes and build them from different materials.

flat

PLEASE READ FIRST: THIS IS A PLAN, NOT A PROMISE

  • The content and the timing will change. This page is what we are aiming to teach and roughly when. Units move, investigations get swapped, and some take longer than planned. What your child is learning about

A scientist is not a person in a white coat. Your child learns to ask a question about something they noticed, look closely, predict, sort, compare, and record what they found. Every session has children doing something, never just listening.

New words: scientist, observe, question, predict, sort, compare, record,

hand lens, evidence

When your child notices something odd, resist explaining it. Ask what they think, and how we could find out.

Five senses, five body parts, a job for each. After this unit no observation is asked for without naming the sense doing the work: which sense will tell us? That question then runs for the rest of the year.

New words: senses, sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch, rough, smooth, loud,

sweet, sour, salty, bitter

At dinner, ask which sense told them the food was ready. More than one answer is right.

What your child is learning about

Sun, Light, and Shadows

The sun lights what it reaches and warms what it touches, and anything it cannot reach is cooler and has a shadow behind it. The class builds a shadow clock outside and watches one shadow move across a whole day.

New words: sun, sunlight, light, block, shadow, shade, warmer, cooler,

longer, shorter

Try this at home

Stand in the same spot in the morning and again in the afternoon, and look at what the shadow did.

Late October to late NovemberUnit 4

What your child is learning about

  • Read longer words by breaking them into parts
  • Write several sentences that belong together
  • Use capital letters, full stops and question marks

Grade 1 · Mathematics

Grade 1 Mathematics

What your child will learn this year, month by month

2026 – 2027

PLEASE READ THIS FIRST

This guide is a plan, not a promise. The months are approximate and dates shift for holidays, snow days, and topics that need more time. There is a topic test roughly every two to three weeks, always with a review day the day before. The timing and some detail will change. Where your child is going by June will not.

Anything that changes will be emailed to you or posted on Class Dojo. Test dates live there, not on this sheet.

Late Aug & SeptPatterns, and what adding means

What your child is learning

  • Finding, continuing and making patterns
  • What adding and taking away actually mean
  • Solving a story problem by drawing it
  • Adding and subtracting within 10

Words to know: pattern · add · subtract · sum · difference · equal

Try this at home

Make a pattern with spoons or socks and ask what comes next. Patterns are everywhere at home.

OctoberKnowing the facts by heart

What your child is learning

  • Adding and subtracting within 10 quickly, without counting
  • Doubles, and making a ten
  • Addition facts up to 20 using strategies

Words to know: doubles · make a ten · strategy · fact · symmetry

Try this at home

Two minutes of quick facts while walking or in the car. Ask out loud, do not write.

NovemberSubtraction facts to 20

What your child is learning

  • Taking away from numbers up to 20
  • Using addition to work out a subtraction
  • Choosing a strategy that fits the numbers
  • Explaining how they got the answer

Words to know: take away · count back · related facts · strategy

Try this at home

Ask how they worked it out, not just what the answer is. The method is what is being taught.

DecemberNumber sentences

What your child is learning

  • The equals sign, and what it really means
  • Finding the missing number in a sentence like 6 + __ = 10
  • Writing a number sentence to match a story

Words to know: equation · equals · missing number · number sentence

Try this at home

Say a number sentence with a gap and let them fill it. Six plus what makes ten.

JanuaryGraphs, and counting to 120

What your child is learning

  • Asking a question that needs counting to answer
  • Making and reading a simple graph
  • Counting all the way to 120
  • Counting by 2s, 5s and 10s

Words to know: data · graph · tally · count on · more than

Try this at home

Count something real together and make a picture graph of it. Socks, shoes, anything.

FebruaryTens and ones

What your child is learning

  • What the two digits in a number each mean
  • Building numbers from tens and ones
  • Comparing two numbers and saying which is larger

Words to know: tens · ones · digit · greater than · less than

Try this at home

Ask how many tens and how many ones are in a number you see. House numbers work well.

MarchAdding tens and ones

What your child is learning

  • Adding a two digit number and a one digit number
  • Adding tens onto a number: 34 and 20
  • Using what they know about tens to work faster
  • Checking whether an answer is sensible

Words to know: add tens · add ones · regroup · estimate

Try this at home

Ask what ten more than a number is. Then ten less. Quick, out loud, often.

AprilSubtracting tens, and measuring

What your child is learning

  • Taking tens away from a two digit number
  • Measuring how long something is
  • Comparing weight, and how much a container holds
  • Estimating a measurement before checking it

Words to know: subtract tens · longer · shorter · heavier · holds more

Try this at home

Measure things with a ruler or with hands and feet. Ask for a guess before you measure.

MayTime, the calendar, and shapes

What your child is learning

  • Telling time to the hour and the half hour
  • Counting coins, and the days and months of the year
  • Naming flat shapes and solid shapes
  • Putting shapes together to make new ones

Words to know: hour · half past · coin · month · square · cube

Try this at home

Ask the time from a real clock with hands, not a phone. Count coins at the shop.

JuneEqual parts

What your child is learning

  • Splitting a shape into halves and fourths
  • Sharing a group fairly between people
  • A final look back over the whole year

Words to know: half · fourth · equal parts · fair share

Try this at home

Cut food into equal parts and name them. Halves and fourths at the table beat any worksheet.

BY JUNE, WITH PRACTICE AT HOME

School lessons alone will not get your child here. A few minutes of number talk at home every day is what makes the rest possible.

  • Add and subtract within 20 without counting on fingers
  • Count to 120, and by 2s, 5s and 10s
  • Say how many tens and ones are in a two digit number
  • Compare two numbers and say which is larger
  • Tell time to the hour and half hour, and count coins
  • Measure and compare length, weight and how much something holds
  • Split a shape into halves and fourths

WHICH IS WHY THESE SIX THINGS MATTER

  • Two to five minutes of quick facts every single day. This is the one that matters most.
  • Ask out loud rather than setting written work.
  • Ask how they worked it out, not just the answer.
  • Count real things together, often.
  • Let them handle coins, a ruler and a clock with hands.
  • If it is getting hard, tell the teacher in October, not April.

Questions are always welcome. Please speak to your child’s teacher or the school office.

Grade 1 · Science

Grade 1 Science

What your child will learn this year, month by month

2026 – 2027

PLEASE READ THIS FIRST

This guide is a plan, not a promise. Science meets twice a week for about forty minutes, so a single closure costs a whole lesson and the months are approximate. Several units run an investigation that takes several weeks, such as growing a plant or keeping a weather chart. The timing and some detail will change. Where your child is going by June will not.

Anything that changes will be emailed to you or posted on Class Dojo. Assessment dates live there, not on this sheet.

Late Aug & SeptHow things move

What your child is learning

  • Asking questions a scientist can actually answer
  • Movement: straight, circular, spinning, back and forth
  • How something shaking makes a sound
  • Building a device that moves an object, then improving it

Words to know: observe · predict · motion · vibration · sound

Try this at home

Ask how something is moving, not just that it is. Straight, round, or back and forth.

OctoberWhat things are made of

What your child is learning

  • Describing a material: color, texture, smell, weight, size
  • Why a thing is made of the material it is made of
  • Materials that let all, some, or no light through
  • Designing a screen or covering that blocks light

Words to know: material · property · texture · weight · light

Try this at home

Ask why a window is glass and a coat is not. Properties and uses go together.

NovemberPlants

What your child is learning

  • What a plant needs to stay alive
  • Roots, stems, leaves, flowers and seeds, and what each does
  • Growing a plant and measuring it over several weeks
  • Sorting plants by what you can see

Words to know: roots · stem · leaf · seed · grow · measure

Try this at home

Grow something on a windowsill and check it together each week. Seeds, beans, anything.

DecemberAnimals

What your child is learning

  • What animals need, including us
  • How legs, wings, fins, fur and feathers help an animal survive
  • Why an animal can live in one place and not another
  • Sorting animals by what you can see

Words to know: needs · survive · covering · environment · classify

Try this at home

Pick any animal and ask what its body helps it do. Works at the zoo, in a book, or out the window.

JanuaryComparing, then the sun

What your child is learning

  • Putting plants and animals side by side
  • How sunlight warms the land, the air and the water
  • Where the sun is in the sky at different times of day
  • A mid-year check on everything since August

Words to know: compare · sunlight · warm · shadow · pattern

Try this at home

Notice which side of the house is warm in the morning and which in the afternoon. Ask why.

FebruaryThe sun, then weather

What your child is learning

  • Keeping a safe sun diary and spotting the pattern
  • Designing something that keeps a place cooler
  • Describing weather: light, temperature and rain
  • Building a class weather chart

Words to know: temperature · shade · weather · record · graph

Try this at home

Check the weather together each morning and say it out loud. Thirty seconds, every day.

MarchWeather and seasons

What your child is learning

  • Reading the class weather chart and finding the pattern
  • How temperature, light and rain change with the seasons
  • Working out the season from the evidence
  • What the seasons do to plants, animals and people

Words to know: season · pattern · evidence · change · graph

Try this at home

Ask what has changed outside since last month. Trees, light, clothes, all of it counts.

AprilNatural resources

What your child is learning

  • Where the things we use actually come from
  • Why many resources are limited
  • How our choices affect what is left
  • Reducing, reusing and recycling, and telling them apart

Words to know: resource · limited · reduce · reuse · recycle · conserve

Try this at home

Pick one thing to waste less of at home for a week. Then ask whether it worked.

MayPutting it into practice

What your child is learning

  • Running a real conservation project and measuring the result
  • Explaining what they found and why they think it
  • A task that links two units together
  • Reviewing the science journal from the whole year

Words to know: plan · result · claim · explain · journal

Try this at home

Ask them to tell you what their project found. Explaining it out loud is where it lands.

JuneLooking back

What your child is learning

  • A final check across everything from August to June
  • Looking through the science journal from the start of the year
  • Reflecting on what they can do now that they could not then
  • Sharing their favorite work

Words to know: review · reflect · portfolio · evidence

Try this at home

Ask what they will remember most from science this year, and why that one.

THE SCIENCE JOURNAL

Your child keeps one science journal from the first week of school to the last. Drawings, measurements, predictions and what actually happened all go in it, and it is reviewed at mid-year and again in June so they can see their own progress. If your child shows you a page from it, ask what they drew and what they found out rather than whether it is neat. In Grade 1 science, recording what you really saw matters more than how tidy the page looks.

BY JUNE, WITH PRACTICE AT HOME

School alone will not get your child here. Twice a week for forty minutes is very little time, so noticing science outside the classroom matters more here than almost anywhere.

  • Describe how something is moving, and connect shaking to sound
  • Describe a material and say why it suits its job
  • Name what plants and animals need to live
  • Say what a root, a leaf, a wing or a fur coat is for
  • Explain how sunlight warms things, and read a weather chart
  • Name a natural resource and say why it is limited
  • Record what they observed, and say what it shows

WHICH IS WHY THESE FIVE THINGS MATTER

  • Ask what they learned in science this week.
  • Ask how they know. Every time.
  • Let them explain something to you. Explaining it out loud is how it sticks.
  • Say you do not know when you do not, then find out together.
  • Notice science outside: puddles drying, shadows moving, seeds sprouting.

Questions are always welcome. Please speak to your child’s teacher or the school office.

Grade 1 · Social Studies

Grade 1 Social Studies

The Commonwealth of Virginia, month by month

2026 – 2027

PLEASE READ THIS FIRST

This guide is a plan, not a promise. Grade 1 Social Studies is about Virginia, our own state, and meets twice a week on Mondays and Fridays for 40 minutes, so a single closure costs a whole lesson and the months are approximate. There is a short checkpoint about every three weeks, given orally. The timing and some detail will change. Where your child is going by June will not.

Anything that changes will be emailed to you or posted on Class Dojo. Assessment dates live there, not on this sheet.

Late Aug & SeptHistorians and good citizens

What your child is learning

  • What a historian does: asking questions and looking at evidence
  • Classroom rules, taking turns, and voting together
  • Why communities set aside special days to remember people
  • The first of twelve holidays we study across the year

Words to know: historian · evidence · citizen · rule · vote · holiday

Try this at home

Ask how they know something, not just what they know. That question is the whole subject.

OctoberHolidays we honor

What your child is learning

  • Holidays that remember people, and holidays that remember events
  • Why different communities honor different days
  • Labor Day, Columbus Day and Indigenous Peoples’ Day, Yorktown
  • Listening respectfully when a tradition is not our own

Words to know: tradition · honor · remember · community · celebrate

Try this at home

When a holiday comes up, ask what it remembers and why people mark it. One question is enough.

NovemberSymbols and what they stand for

What your child is learning

  • The American flag and the Virginia flag
  • The bald eagle, the Capitol, and other national symbols
  • The Pledge of Allegiance and the national anthem
  • Election Day and Veterans Day, in the month they fall

Words to know: symbol · flag · pledge · anthem · patriotism

Try this at home

Spot a flag or a symbol on a coin and ask what it stands for. They are everywhere once you look.

DecemberMaps and globes

What your child is learning

  • What a map is for, and how a globe is different
  • Using a map key and the four directions
  • Finding Virginia, and finding where we live on it
  • Drawing a map of a place they know

Words to know: map · globe · key · north · south · east · west

Try this at home

Look at a map before you drive somewhere. Ask which direction you are heading.

JanuaryMaps, then Virginia’s land

What your child is learning

  • Finishing the map unit with a make-a-map project
  • Virginia’s mountains, valleys, rivers and coast
  • Where the water is, and why it matters
  • How the land shapes the way people live and work

Words to know: landform · mountain · valley · river · coast · region

Try this at home

Ask what the land looks like where we live, and how it is different somewhere else in Virginia.

FebruaryVirginia’s seasons

What your child is learning

  • The four seasons and how each one changes daily life
  • What people wear, grow and do in each season here
  • Comparing our part of Virginia with another part
  • The longest unit of the year, finishing before spring break

Words to know: season · weather · climate · crops · compare

Try this at home

Talk about what changes at home with the seasons. Clothes, food, daylight, all of it counts.

MarchVirginia long ago

What your child is learning

  • The Indigenous people who lived in Virginia first
  • Werowocomoco, and life before the English arrived
  • Jamestown, the first permanent English settlement
  • How Virginia’s communities have changed over time

Words to know: Indigenous · Werowocomoco · Jamestown · settlement ·

change

Try this at home

Ask what surprised them. Most children assume the story starts with Jamestown, and it does not.

AprilNeeds, wants and money

What your child is learning

  • The difference between goods and services
  • What we need against what we want
  • Earning money, spending it, and saving it
  • The unit ends with a class Market Day

Words to know: goods · services · needs · wants · earn · save · choice

Try this at home

At the shop, ask whether something is a need or a want. Best five second lesson of the year.

MayVirginians who changed things

What your child is learning

  • Fourteen Virginians who shaped the Commonwealth
  • How one person’s idea can change a whole community
  • People from many backgrounds and many times
  • What made each of them worth remembering

Words to know: changemaker · contribution · community · courage

Try this at home

Ask which changemaker they would want to meet, and why. The reason matters more than the name.

JuneOur Commonwealth showcase

What your child is learning

  • Putting together a portfolio built up across the whole year
  • Presenting what they have learned out loud
  • Looking back over everything since August
  • The year ends with the Commonwealth Showcase

Words to know: Commonwealth · portfolio · showcase · present

Try this at home

Let them practice their showcase presentation on you. Saying it out loud twice beats saying it once.

BY JUNE, WITH PRACTICE AT HOME

School alone will not get your child here. Twice a week for 40 minutes is very little time, so talking about it at home matters more in this subject than in almost any other.

  • Say what a good citizen does, and why rules matter
  • Name national and Virginia symbols and what they stand for
  • Read a simple map using a key and the four directions
  • Describe Virginia’s land, water and four seasons
  • Say who lived in Virginia first, and what Jamestown was
  • Tell a need from a want, and a good from a service
  • Name Virginians who changed their community, and how

WHICH IS WHY THESE SIX THINGS MATTER

  • Ask what they learned in Social Studies. Monday or Friday evening is the moment.
  • Ask how they know, not just what happened.
  • Look at maps together. Any map, any time.
  • Talk about needs and wants when you shop.
  • Take them somewhere in Virginia and name what you see.
  • If it is getting hard, tell the teacher in October, not April.

Questions are always welcome. Please speak to your child’s teacher or the school office.

BY JUNE, WITH PRACTICE AT HOME

  • Read a page of a chapter book smoothly
  • Work out a new word by trying a sound and testing it
  • Spell most common words, and make a sensible attempt at the rest
  • Write a piece that was planned, drafted, corrected and copied out
  • Use capitals, full stops, apostrophes and quotation marks correctly
  • Point to the part of the page that proves their answer
  • Use a story book and an information book differently

WHICH IS WHY THESE SIX THINGS MATTER

  • Twenty minutes of reading a day. Regular beats long.
  • They read to you, and you read to them. Both matter.
  • When they stick, count to five before helping. The learning is in that pause.
  • Practice spelling a little each night, not all the night before.
  • Ask what happened, or what they found out. Then ask how they know.
  • If it is getting hard, tell the teacher in October, not April.

Grade 2 Science

Science meets twice a week for 40 minutes, and lessons begin the week of September 14. We use Five Ponds Press, Exploring Science All Around Us, Level 2, and we follow the Virginia Science Standards of Learning for Grade 2. There is a science worksheet once every two weeks, which comes home inside the weekly packet. There is one test at the end of each unit, and your child will have a review sheet and a review lesson before every one of them.

Mid September to late October

What your child is learning

Forces and Motion, and Thinking Like a Scientist

Your child has already begun this in class, reading Five Ponds Chapter 1, All About Moving, and meeting push, pull, gravity and magnetism. Now the class investigates those ideas properly: predicting before testing, measuring how far a toy car travels in inches after a gentle push and after a hard one, sorting which materials a magnet pulls toward it, and showing that gravity and magnets both work without touching. The unit ends with each child designing a small tool that uses a push, a pull or a magnet to solve a classroom problem. Alongside the science, this is where the science notebook starts, where the ruler and thermometer are taught, and where two routines begin that run right through to June: a Daily Weather Watch chart, and a Seasonal Change Journal in which the class draws the same tree at school six times between September and June.

New words: force, push, pull, magnet, attract, gravity, observe, measure

Try this at home

Look at your fridge magnets together. Ask what else in the house a magnet would stick to, then test a few and see who was right.

Late October to late November

What your child is learning

Matter: Solids, Liquids and Gases

Matter is anything that has weight and takes up space, and that includes air. Solids keep their own shape, liquids take the shape of the container, gases fill the whole container. Heating and cooling change water between ice, liquid water and water vapor, and it stays water the whole time.

New words: matter, solid, liquid, gas, melt, freeze

Try this at home

Pour the same cup of water into three differently shaped containers. Ask whether there is more, less or the same amount each time.

Early December to mid January

What your child is learning

Weather on Earth

The four things we measure: temperature, cloud cover, precipitation and wind. The class builds a simple rain gauge and a wind vane, then graphs the weather data it has been collecting since September and finds the pattern. Storms follow, then drought and flooding. The unit closes with each child writing a forecast and saying what people should do to get ready for it.

New words: thermometer, rain gauge, precipitation, forecast, drought, flood

Try this at home

Watch a weather forecast together. Ask your child what people should do to get ready for it.

Mid January to late February

What your child is learning

Seasons and the Changing Land

How plants rest through winter and grow again in spring, and how animals get through it. Some migrate, some hibernate, and some stay and grow thicker fur or use camouflage. Then how wind and water slowly break rock into smaller pieces and carry soil away, and why plant roots hold soil in place.

New words: season, dormant, migrate, hibernate, camouflage, erosion

Try this at home

Pick a tree near your home in January and look at it again in April. Talk about everything that changed.

Mid March to early April

What your child is learning

Life Cycles of Plants and Animals

Every living thing is born, grows, makes new living things and dies. Some baby animals look like their parents and some do not at all. The butterfly and the frog life cycles, then the plant life cycle from seed to flower to new seed. Each child plants bean seeds in class at the start of the unit and measures them as it goes on.

New words: life cycle, egg, larva, pupa, tadpole, seed, germinate

Try this at home

Save a seed from fruit at home. Ask your child to name the stages the plant would go through.

Mid April to early May

What your child is learning

Habitats and Living Things

The five things a habitat has to give an animal: food, water, air, shelter and space, and what happens to the animal if even one is missing. How plants and animals depend on each other. How habitats change, both through storms and seasons and through people building roads and houses. Each child builds a labeled model of a habitat.

New words: habitat, living, nonliving, shelter, depend

Try this at home

Look under a stone or a leaf outside together. Talk about what that small creature finds in the place where it lives.

Mid May to early June

What your child is learning

Plants as Natural Resources

How much comes to us from plants: paper, cotton, wood, rubber, spices and some medicines. Which crops grow well in Virginia and why the soil and the weather decide it. How plants feed and shelter animals, put oxygen into the air we breathe, and hold the soil against wind and water.

New words: natural resource, crop, oxygen, windbreak

Try this at home

Open a kitchen cupboard together and find three things that came from a plant. Some will surprise you both.

June

What your child is learning

A Year of Change, and the Science Showcase

The class looks back over the whole year, and each child prepares one investigation to present, alongside the Daily Weather Watch graph running from September to June. The year closes with a Grade 2 Science Showcase.

New words: investigation, showcase

Try this at home

Ask your child to teach you one thing they found out this year, and to tell you how they found it out.

Describe a push, a pull, gravity and magnetism, and give an everyday example of each. Sort things into solids, liquids and gases, and explain how heating and cooling change them. Read a thermometer, describe the weather in four ways, and explain a pattern in weather data. Explain how plants and animals respond when the seasons change, and how wind and water change the land. Describe the life cycle of a plant and the life cycle of an animal, and say what they have in common. Name the five things a habitat gives an animal, and say what happens when a habitat changes. Explain why plants matter, to people and to animals.

When the science worksheet comes home, read the questions aloud if your child needs it, but let your child write the answers. 5. Let your child be wrong first and then find out. Guessing, testing and correcting is how science is actually done, and it is not a mistake.

THE MULTIPLICATION FACTS, IN THE ORDER WE TEACH THEM

The class does not learn the times tables in order from one to twelve. Working on the 7s in December, before the easy facts are secure, makes it harder rather than faster. Five minutes a day, every day, from November onwards is the single most useful thing you can do for Grade 3 math.

  • Know the multiplication and division facts within 100
  • Find the area and perimeter of a shape
  • Name and compare fractions, and place them on a number line
  • Read a bar graph, measure, and work out elapsed time
  • Explain which strategy they chose and why

WHICH IS WHY THESE FIVE THINGS MATTER

  • Five minutes of facts every single day, in the order the class is learning them.
  • Ask out loud rather than setting extra written work.
  • Ask how they worked it out, not just the answer.
  • Ask for an estimate before the real answer.

THE TWO HABITS THAT RUN ALL YEAR

The facts. Grade 5 mathematics assumes every multiplication and division fact through twelve times twelve is instant. It is not taught again this year, it is used. A child still counting to find seven times eight will struggle with long division, with fractions and with decimals, and will run out of time in a test. Five minutes a night at home is the single most useful thing you can do. Estimate before you calculate. Virginia asks Grade 5 students to estimate first, then check the real answer against the estimate. So when you help, ask roughly how much before any working out. It is not a shortcut, it is part of what is being graded.

BY JUNE, WITH PRACTICE AT HOME

  • Say whether a number up to 100 is prime, and give its prime factors.
  • Add, subtract, multiply and divide decimals.
  • Multiply three digits by two digits, and divide by two digits.
  • Add and subtract fractions with different denominators.
  • Use the order of operations correctly.
  • Find the area of a right triangle and the volume of a box.
  • Measure an angle and find the missing angle in a triangle.
  • Find the mean, median, mode and range of a set of data.

Classroom lessons alone will not get your child there. The children who can do all nine are the ones who practiced at home.

WHICH IS WHY THESE FIVE THINGS MATTER

  • Five minutes of multiplication and division facts every single day.
  • Ask for an estimate before the real answer, every time.
  • Ask how they worked it out, not just what the answer is.
  • Do the review sheet with them the night before a test.
  • If it is getting hard, tell the teacher in October, not April.

Grades 7 and 8 Physical Science

A family guide to the year, month by month

This is a plan, not a promise. These are the overall topics for each month. Order and timing will change as the year goes on, and months are approximate. Where your child lands in June does not change.

Anything that changes is emailed to you or posted on Class Dojo. The test dates below are the plan as it stands today; when the calendar moves, the real dates are posted there.

Physical Science is one class for Grades 7 and 8 and meets five periods a week. Your child works from Savvas Experience Science with laboratory work built around it, following the Virginia Physical Science standards.

Late August and SeptemberDoing science, then atoms

What your child is learning

  • Topics: How a result is made trustworthy: variables, repeated trials, measurement and graphing. Then the atom, its particles, and how the model was rebuilt as new evidence arrived.
  • In the lab: A measurement carousel, an investigation showing how a single trial misleads, a design challenge, and a gold foil simulation.
  • Handed in: The lab safety practical in early September, which must be passed before any Unit 2 lab work, and a design-and-test task.

Words to know: variable, control, trial, accuracy, precision, atom, proton, neutron, electron, isotope

Try this at home

Ask why one trial is not enough. If your child can explain that, the first unit has landed.

OctoberStates of matter, then the periodic table

What your child is learning

  • Topics: Why a gas fills its container and a solid does not, what temperature measures, then reading the periodic table and predicting what an element will do from its position.
  • In the lab: Particle motion simulations, pressure demonstrations, and a mystery element sort where students build their own table before seeing the real one.
  • Handed in: A model evaluation task and the Unit 2 assessment, then an element prediction task. Quarter 1 closes Oct 23.

Words to know: kinetic molecular theory, group, family, period, valence electron, metal, metalloid, nonmetal

Try this at home

Point at anything in the kitchen and ask which elements are in it, then where they sit on the table.

NovemberProperties, changes and bonding

What your child is learning

  • Topics: Identifying a substance from properties that do not depend on how much you have, telling a physical change from a chemical one, and why ionic and covalent bonds form.
  • In the lab: A density column and unknown identification lab, and ionic and covalent samples compared by conductivity and melting point.
  • Handed in: Classification checks with evidence required, bond-type predictions, and balancing practice sets twice a week.

Words to know: density, solubility, physical change, chemical change, ion, ionic bond, covalent bond

Try this at home

Ask whether cooking an egg is a physical or a chemical change, and make them defend the answer.

DecemberConservation of matter

Grade 8 · Physical Science

Grades 7 and 8 Physical Science

A family guide to the year, month by month

THIS PLAN IS TENTATIVE

This is a plan, not a promise. These are the overall topics for each month. Order and timing will change as the year goes on, and months are approximate. Where your child lands in June does not change.

Anything that changes is emailed to you or posted on Class Dojo. The test dates below are the plan as it stands today; when the calendar moves, the real dates are posted there.

Physical Science is one class for Grades 7 and 8 and meets five periods a week. Your child works from Savvas Experience Science with laboratory work built around it, following the Virginia Physical Science standards.

Late August and SeptemberDoing science, then atoms

What your child is learning

  • Topics: How a result is made trustworthy: variables, repeated trials, measurement and graphing. Then the atom, its particles, and how the model was rebuilt as new evidence arrived.
  • In the lab: A measurement carousel, an investigation showing how a single trial misleads, a design challenge, and a gold foil simulation.
  • Handed in: The lab safety practical in early September, which must be passed before any Unit 2 lab work, and a design-and-test task.

Words to know: variable, control, trial, accuracy, precision, atom, proton, neutron, electron, isotope

Try this at home

Ask why one trial is not enough. If your child can explain that, the first unit has landed.

OctoberStates of matter, then the periodic table

What your child is learning

  • Topics: Why a gas fills its container and a solid does not, what temperature measures, then reading the periodic table and predicting what an element will do from its position.
  • In the lab: Particle motion simulations, pressure demonstrations, and a mystery element sort where students build their own table before seeing the real one.
  • Handed in: A model evaluation task and the Unit 2 assessment, then an element prediction task. Quarter 1 closes Oct 23.

Words to know: kinetic molecular theory, group, family, period, valence electron, metal, metalloid, nonmetal

Try this at home

Point at anything in the kitchen and ask which elements are in it, then where they sit on the table.

NovemberProperties, changes and bonding

What your child is learning

  • Topics: Identifying a substance from properties that do not depend on how much you have, telling a physical change from a chemical one, and why ionic and covalent bonds form.
  • In the lab: A density column and unknown identification lab, and ionic and covalent samples compared by conductivity and melting point.
  • Handed in: Classification checks with evidence required, bond-type predictions, and balancing practice sets twice a week.

Words to know: density, solubility, physical change, chemical change, ion, ionic bond, covalent bond

Try this at home

Ask whether cooking an egg is a physical or a chemical change, and make them defend the answer.
